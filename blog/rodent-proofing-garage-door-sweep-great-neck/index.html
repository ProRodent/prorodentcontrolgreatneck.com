A garage door's bottom seal, usually a strip of rubber or vinyl, wears down over years of regular use, and the gap it leaves behind grows slowly enough that most households never notice until a rodent problem traces directly back to it. This is one of the most common entry points we find across Great Neck homes, regardless of house age.

How small a gap actually matters.

A house mouse can fit through a gap roughly a quarter inch wide, about the diameter of a pencil. A worn garage door sweep can develop a gap well beyond that without looking obviously broken, especially at the corners where wear tends to concentrate first. A visual check from a few feet away often misses a gap that size entirely.

Norway rats need a slightly larger gap, closer to half an inch, but a sweep worn enough to let a mouse through is often not far from letting a rat through as well, especially at a worn corner rather than the center of the door.

Where wear concentrates first.

  • The corners of the door, where the sweep flexes most with each use.
  • Anywhere the driveway surface is uneven, creating an inconsistent seal.
  • Older rubber sweeps that have hardened and cracked with age.
  • A sweep that was never replaced since the home's original construction.
A garage door gap grows slowly enough that most people never notice it happening.

What a proper fix actually looks like.

Replacing a worn sweep with a new, properly fitted one closes most of the gap, but fit matters more than people expect, since an uneven driveway surface can leave a new sweep gapping at one end even when installed correctly. A technician checks the full width of the door, not just the center, and may recommend a bulb-style or double-fin sweep for a driveway with noticeable unevenness.

A gap can persist even after a sweep replacement, due to an uneven slab or a warped door panel. Additional sealing along that specific gap, using the same hardware cloth or mesh approach used elsewhere during rodent exclusion and entry point sealing, closes what the sweep alone cannot.

Why this matters more for a garage used for storage.

A garage that holds long-term storage, boxes, tools, holiday items, gives a mouse that gets through the door seal somewhere to settle in rather than passing through to look elsewhere. Checking what is stored and whether it is in sealed containers rather than cardboard reduces the appeal even if a small gap exists.

How to check your own garage door sweep.

With the door closed, a flashlight held outside at ground level while someone checks from inside for visible light coming through gives a rough sense of whether gaps exist, particularly at the corners. This is not a substitute for a professional check but can flag an obvious problem before scheduling a visit.

How often sweeps actually need replacing.

There is no fixed schedule, since wear depends on usage frequency, driveway surface and the sweep material itself. A visual and tactile check during any rodent inspection catches a worn sweep well before it becomes an active entry point, which is one reason garage checks are a standard part of garage and basement rodent control.

The side and top seals matter too.

A garage door's bottom sweep gets most of the attention, reasonably, since it sees the most wear. But the vertical seals along each side of the door frame and the seal along the top header can also develop gaps, particularly on an older door that has shifted slightly on its track over years of use. These side seals are easy to overlook since they are vertical rather than along the ground, where most people instinctively check first.

A door that closes slightly unevenly, visibly tighter on one side than the other, is worth a closer look at both the affected side seal and the track alignment itself. That unevenness is often what allows a gap to develop somewhere other than the bottom sweep.

What about a garage with no interior door to the house.

A detached garage, common on some Great Neck properties, does not carry the same risk of a sealed garage directly connecting to living space. It still deserves the same sweep and seal attention, since an unsealed detached garage can become a staging area where rodents establish themselves before eventually finding their way toward the main house through an entirely separate gap later on. Treating a detached structure as lower priority because it is not attached to the house is a common and understandable mistake, but not an accurate one.

Safe handling checklist for droppings and nests

These steps follow CDC cleanup guidance for rodent droppings and nests. They are general safety practice for any household.

  • Skip the broom and the vacuum on dry droppings. Stirring them up is how dust gets airborne.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Common questions

Can I replace a garage door sweep myself?

Yes, it is a straightforward DIY task for many homeowners, though an uneven driveway surface can make a proper fit trickier than expected.

How do I know if my sweep is actually worn enough to matter?

A flashlight check at ground level with the door closed, looking for visible light from outside, gives a rough sense, though an inspection measures it more precisely.

Does a new sweep guarantee the garage is rodent-proof?

It closes the most common gap, but a full inspection checks other garage entry points too, vents, utility penetrations and the garage-to-house connection.
