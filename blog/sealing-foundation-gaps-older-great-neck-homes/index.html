Many older houses sit on masonry or block foundations built before modern construction standards. Decades of normal settling on these older foundations create gaps that a newer poured-concrete slab does not develop at the same rate, and those gaps are among the most common rodent entry points on older houses.

What settling actually does to a foundation.

Soil shifts slightly with seasonal moisture changes, and a foundation built decades ago was often constructed with mortar and masonry techniques that flex and crack more than a modern poured slab does. Over enough winters and summers, hairline cracks widen, mortar joints between masonry blocks loosen, and small gaps develop around where pipes or cables pass through the foundation wall.

None of this means an older home was built poorly. It means older materials behave differently over a long timeline than the standards used in a more recent renovation, and inspecting for that difference is part of what makes a thorough visit effective.

Where these gaps typically show up.

  • Mortar joints between masonry foundation blocks.
  • Around older utility penetrations for water, gas or electrical lines.
  • At the sill plate where the wood framing meets the masonry foundation.
  • Along foundation vents that have warped or rusted over time.
A gap that took decades to open does not get fixed with a quick bead of caulk.

Why caulk is not the right material here.

Standard caulk is soft enough that both mice and rats can chew through it within days to weeks, which means a caulked gap often looks sealed for a short time before reopening. For a genuine older-foundation gap, steel wool packed tightly into smaller openings, galvanized hardware cloth fitted over larger gaps or vents, and actual mortar patching for masonry cracks are the materials that hold up to repeated chewing attempts.

Mortar patching in particular takes longer to do correctly than a quick caulk application, since it needs to cure properly and match the surrounding masonry, but it is the only approach that actually restores the foundation's integrity rather than just covering the gap temporarily.

What a sealing visit on an older home actually involves.

A technician walks the full foundation perimeter, not just the side where activity was reported, since settling rarely affects only one side of a structure evenly. Every mortar joint, vent and utility penetration gets checked, and each one found gets the material matched to the size and type of gap, not a single generic fix applied everywhere.

This is why rodent exclusion and entry point sealing follows a full rodent inspection and estimates visit rather than being quoted sight unseen. An older foundation with several small gaps needs a different scope than a newer home with one obvious opening.

How this differs for more recently renovated homes.

A home that has had foundation or siding work done more recently in Great Neck usually has fewer of these settling-related gaps, since the newer materials and techniques used in a renovation address a lot of what an older, untouched foundation develops over time. When we do find a gap on a recently renovated property, it is more often a single missed detail, a vent cover removed during work and never replaced correctly, rather than widespread wear.

Confirming the seal actually holds.

A follow-up visit checks that mortar has cured properly and that mesh or steel wool placements are still intact, since a hard winter or a storm can occasionally stress a recent repair. This is a standard part of the process on older-foundation jobs specifically, given how much more work the foundation has typically needed.

What homeowners sometimes get wrong about older-home sealing.

A common misconception is that sealing an older foundation is a one-time project that, once finished, never needs attention again. In reality, a foundation that developed gaps over decades of settling can, on rare occasion, develop a new one in a different spot years later, particularly after an unusually harsh winter freeze-thaw cycle. This is not a sign the original sealing work failed, it is simply a different section of an aging structure behaving the way aging structures do.

Keeping a rough record of what was sealed and when makes it much easier to tell a genuinely new gap apart from a previously sealed one that might need a quick recheck. We provide that record as part of the documentation on any exclusion visit.

A note on historic-designation considerations.

If a property carries a historic designation or sits in a recognized historic district, that can affect what exterior changes are permitted. Mortar patching and mesh fitted inside existing openings generally does not require special approval the way an exterior facade change would. If a property has this kind of designation, though, mentioning it on the call lets us plan sealing work that respects those constraints from the start.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Wet droppings with a disinfectant or a bleach solution and let it soak for about five minutes before wiping with paper towels.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • If you develop fever, muscle aches or trouble breathing in the weeks after cleaning up rodent droppings, tell a doctor about the exposure. We are not medical professionals and cannot judge individual health risk.

Common questions

How do I know if my foundation has these gaps without an inspection?

A visual walk of the exterior foundation line can spot obvious cracks or gaps, but many entry points are small enough to miss without a trained eye checking specifically for rodent access.

Is mortar patching more expensive than caulking?

It typically takes more time to do correctly, but caulk fails against chewing within weeks, making mortar or mesh the only approach that actually lasts.

Will sealing affect my foundation's structural integrity?

No, proper sealing restores small gaps without affecting the foundation's overall structure. It addresses rodent access, not structural repair, which is a separate matter if ever needed.
