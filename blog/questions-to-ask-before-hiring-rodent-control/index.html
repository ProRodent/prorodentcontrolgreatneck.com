Hiring rodent control sight unseen, often while dealing with an active and stressful problem, is not the moment most people want to be doing careful research. A short list of direct questions asked up front, though, can tell you a lot about whether a company's approach is actually thorough or just a quick trap-and-leave visit.

Does the price come before or after an inspection?

A company that quotes a firm price over the phone without seeing the property is either guessing or planning to adjust the price once they arrive, which leads to surprises either way. The more honest approach is an inspection first, with the plan and price explained together based on what was actually found, which is how rodent inspection and estimates works here.

Does the plan include sealing, or just trapping?

Trapping removes what is already inside a house. It does nothing about the entry point that let the animal in to begin with, which means a trapping-only visit often sees the same problem return within a season. Ask specifically whether rodent exclusion and entry point sealing is included or treated as a separate add-on, and whether that distinction is explained clearly.

  • Is there a follow-up visit included, and what does it check?
  • Is damage repair priced separately, or folded into one number?
  • Is the address inside the company's actual confirmed coverage area?
  • Is the line answered 24/7, or only during business hours?
A trap-only visit without sealing is a temporary fix dressed up as a permanent one.

Is damage repair priced honestly and separately?

If chewed insulation, wiring or ductwork is found during a visit, that repair work should be documented and priced as its own line item, not quietly folded into the main service cost where it is hard to tell what you are actually paying for. A company that gives you a clear breakdown is giving you something you can actually evaluate.

What does the follow-up visit actually check?

Ask specifically what a follow-up visit covers: are traps checked and reset, are sealed entry points inspected for whether they held, is there a defined point at which the job is considered complete? A vague answer here often means the follow-up is more of a formality than an actual check.

Is your address actually in their coverage area?

Some companies will take a call from outside their realistic service radius and either show up late, charge a steep travel fee, or simply not show up reliably. Asking directly whether your ZIP code is in their confirmed coverage area, and getting a specific answer rather than a vague yes, avoids this entirely.

Are children and pets specifically accounted for?

If there are children or pets in the home, ask how bait stations and traps will be placed to account for that. A company that has a clear, specific answer, rather than a general reassurance, is more likely to actually follow through carefully on site.

Should you get more than one estimate?

For a straightforward mouse call, getting a second opinion rarely changes much, since the scope tends to be fairly predictable once an inspection happens. For a larger job, a suspected rat infestation with yard-side burrows, or a commercial account with recurring monitoring needs, a second inspection and estimate is a reasonable step if the first one left you uncertain, particularly around pricing for damage repair or exclusion work.

What matters most when comparing two estimates is comparing like for like. Does each one include the same scope, inspection, trapping, sealing and documented damage repair, or is one quote a narrower trapping-only visit priced to look cheaper on paper while leaving out work the other estimate actually includes.

A quote that looks unusually low compared to another is worth a closer read. Before assuming it is simply the better deal, check what is and is not actually included, since a narrower scope dressed up to look like a full solution is a known way prices get undercut in this industry.

What a straight answer sounds like on the phone.

A company confident in its process answers these questions directly and specifically on the first call, not with a vague reassurance that sidesteps the actual question asked. If a question about coverage area, pricing order or follow-up scope gets a hedge instead of a clear answer, that hedge is itself useful information worth factoring into the decision.

What this looks like in practice.

On a Great Neck call, this means: an inspection before any firm price, a plan that includes both removal and sealing as distinct steps, damage priced separately if found, a defined follow-up visit, and a straight answer about whether your specific ZIP code is covered. None of this is unusual to ask for, and a company confident in its process should answer all of it without hesitation.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Skip the broom and the vacuum on dry droppings. Stirring them up is how dust gets airborne.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Sources and standards

Common questions

Should I be suspicious of a very low quoted price?

A price quoted before any inspection, high or low, is not based on what your property actually needs. The more useful question is whether an inspection happens before pricing at all.

Is it normal to ask about coverage area directly?

Yes, and a company confident in its service radius should give a clear, specific answer rather than a vague assurance.

What if a company says sealing isn't necessary?

Ask why, specifically. Trapping without sealing the entry point is a known reason problems return, so a company skipping that step should explain the reasoning clearly.
